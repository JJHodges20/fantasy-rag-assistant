TITLE: Middle-earth — Characters and Factions
SERIES: The Lord of the Rings
COVERAGE: The Hobbit, The Lord of the Rings, and relevant wider Middle-earth legendarium
SPOILER LEVEL: FULL
CANON BASIS: J.R.R. Tolkien's published Middle-earth writings

OVERVIEW

The Lord of the Rings follows an alliance of Hobbits, Men, Elves, Dwarves, and other peoples resisting the Dark Lord Sauron. Although powerful rulers and warriors influence the outcome of the War of the Ring, its central mission depends upon the courage of seemingly ordinary individuals. The characters differ in their motivations, their responses to temptation, and their willingness to sacrifice power for the greater good.

THE FELLOWSHIP OF THE RING

Frodo Baggins is a Hobbit of the Shire who inherits the One Ring from Bilbo Baggins. At the Council of Elrond, Frodo volunteers to carry the Ring to Mordor. The burden progressively damages his physical and psychological well-being. Although Frodo reaches Mount Doom, he ultimately claims the Ring instead of destroying it. After its destruction through Gollum's intervention, Frodo returns to the Shire but never fully recovers from his experiences. He eventually sails into the West.

Samwise Gamgee is Frodo's gardener, closest companion, and most faithful protector. Sam repeatedly rescues and encourages Frodo during their journey, briefly carries the One Ring, and helps him reach Mount Doom. After the war, Sam returns home, marries Rosie Cotton, and becomes an important figure in the Shire.

Meriadoc Brandybuck, called Merry, and Peregrin Took, called Pippin, are Hobbits who join the Fellowship. Following their capture by Orcs, they escape into Fangorn Forest and encounter Treebeard. Their involvement helps bring the Ents into conflict with Saruman. Merry later assists Éowyn in defeating the Witch-king, while Pippin serves in Gondor and helps save Faramir.

Aragorn is the heir of Isildur and a descendant of the kings of Númenor. Initially known as the Ranger Strider, he becomes a leader of the Fellowship after Gandalf falls in Moria. Aragorn ultimately claims his inheritance, becoming King Elessar of the Reunited Kingdom of Gondor and Arnor. He marries Arwen, daughter of Elrond.

Gandalf is one of the Istari, or Wizards, sent to aid Middle-earth against Sauron. As a Maia, he possesses spiritual power but is expected to guide rather than dominate others. Gandalf confronts the Balrog in Moria and dies after their struggle. He is sent back as Gandalf the White, subsequently taking a leading role in the defense of the Free Peoples.

Legolas is an Elven prince from the Woodland Realm and a skilled archer. Gimli is a Dwarf, son of Glóin, who represents his people in the Fellowship. Their developing friendship demonstrates the possibility of reconciliation between peoples historically divided by mistrust.

Boromir is the elder son of Denethor II, Steward of Gondor. His desire to protect Gondor makes him vulnerable to the One Ring's temptation. He attempts to take it from Frodo but later repents and dies defending Merry and Pippin from Orcs.

ELVEN LEADERS AND ALLIES

Elrond is the master of Rivendell, a refuge and center of learning. He convenes the council that determines the Ring must be destroyed. He is also the bearer of Vilya, one of the Three Elven Rings.

Galadriel is a powerful Elf who rules Lothlórien alongside Celeborn. She bears Nenya and resists the temptation to accept the One Ring from Frodo. At the end of the Third Age, she departs Middle-earth.

Arwen, Elrond's daughter, chooses a mortal life because of her love for Aragorn. She becomes his queen and survives him, eventually dying in Lothlórien.

THE KINGDOMS OF MEN

Gondor is ruled during the War of the Ring by Denethor II, its Steward. Denethor becomes increasingly consumed by despair and ultimately dies by suicide during the siege of Minas Tirith.

Faramir, Denethor's younger son, differs from Boromir in his response to the Ring. He encounters Frodo in Ithilien but allows him to continue his mission. After the war, Faramir becomes Prince of Ithilien and marries Éowyn.

Rohan is ruled by King Théoden, who initially suffers under the influence of his adviser Gríma Wormtongue and Saruman's schemes. Restored to leadership, Théoden guides the Rohirrim to Gondor's aid and dies during the Battle of the Pelennor Fields.

Éowyn, Théoden's niece, disguises herself as a warrior to ride into battle. Assisted by Merry, she defeats the Witch-king of Angmar. Her brother Éomer succeeds Théoden as King of Rohan.

SAURON AND HIS SERVANTS

Sauron is a fallen Maia who previously served Morgoth. He seeks domination over Middle-earth through military conquest, deception, and the recovery of the One Ring. His defeat occurs when the Ring is destroyed, permanently ending his ability to regain his former power.

The Nazgûl, or Ringwraiths, are nine human rulers or powerful men corrupted through Rings of Power. Their leader, the Witch-king of Angmar, commands Sauron's forces and is defeated during the Battle of the Pelennor Fields.

Saruman is another of the Istari who becomes obsessed with acquiring power and the Ring. From Isengard, he breeds armies, destroys forests, and wages war against Rohan. His power is eventually broken, and he is later killed by Gríma Wormtongue in the Shire.

Gollum was originally Sméagol, a Hobbit-like being corrupted by centuries of possession of the One Ring. He alternates between moments of cooperation and treachery toward Frodo and Sam. At Mount Doom, he bites the Ring from Frodo's hand and falls into the fire while holding it.

OTHER PEOPLES AND FACTIONS

The Dwarves maintain important settlements such as Erebor and the Iron Hills. The Elves inhabit realms including Rivendell, Lothlórien, and the Woodland Realm. The Ents are ancient tree-like shepherds of the forests, led during the war by Treebeard.

Sauron's armies include Orcs, Trolls, and human forces from regions such as Harad and Rhûn. These peoples and armies do not all share the same history or motivations, although many serve Sauron's military campaigns.

The Free Peoples ultimately prevail through cooperation, endurance, and the destruction of the One Ring rather than through military strength alone.

CANON NOTES AND LIMITATIONS

Character portrayals and some events differ between Tolkien's novels and film adaptations. This document follows the published literary narrative, including events such as the Scouring of the Shire.

The identities, histories, and final fates of certain supporting characters are explored more extensively in The Silmarillion, Unfinished Tales, and appendices to The Lord of the Rings. Those works should not be confused with the narrower events of the main trilogy.
